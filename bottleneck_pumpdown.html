Bottleneck-limited pumpdown: viscous vs. molecular flow

A turbopump hangs off a small manifold, connected to the chamber you actually care about through a narrow tube. Near atmosphere the tube conducts like a fire hose and the pump is the limit; once the mean free path outgrows the tube bore, conductance collapses to a fixed geometric value and the tube — not the pump — sets how fast the chamber empties.

Why the bottleneck stops making sense at high vacuum. Gas flow through a tube is characterized by its conductance C (L/s), and the pump speed the chamber actually feels is the series combination S_eff = (1/S + 1/C)⁻¹ — the smaller of the two always wins.
Viscous regime (λ ≪ d): molecules collide with each other constantly, so gas moves as a bulk fluid driven by pressure gradient — Poiseuille flow, C ≈ 180 · d⁴ · P̄ / L (cm, Torr → L/s). Conductance is proportional to pressure and is enormous near atmosphere, so the tube is invisible and the pump is the limit. Your fluid-flow intuition works here.
Molecular regime (λ ≳ d): a molecule crosses the whole tube without meeting another molecule. There is no "flow" to push — each molecule wanders by wall bounces and only by chance finds the exit. Conductance collapses to a pressure-independent, purely geometric value C_mol ≈ 12.1 · d³ / (L + 4d/3), which for a narrow tube can be orders of magnitude below the pump speed. The turbopump can remove everything that arrives, but almost nothing arrives: S_eff → C_mol, no matter how big the pump is. That is why time-to-base scales so brutally with a small bore at high vacuum — and why d⁴ vs d³ matters far less than the loss of the pressure term.
Mean free path for air at 20 °C: λ = 5×10⁻³ / P cm·Torr — the classic "5 microns at 1 Torr". Regime boundaries drawn at Kn = λ/d of 0.01 (viscous) and 0.5 (molecular).
Model: air at 20 °C; two lumped volumes coupled by C(P̄) = C_viscous + C_molecular (Knudsen-interpolation approximation); the pump is a two-stage Edwards station with pressure-dependent speed S(P): an nXDS10iC dry scroll for roughing (peak 11.4 m³/h = 3.17 L/s, ultimate 7×10⁻³ mbar — note the "10iC" is the scroll, not a turbo) handing off near ~0.5–1 Torr to an nEXT85-class turbo (84 L/s N₂ plateau at DN63, critical backing 18 mbar; the Turbo-plateau slider rescales this curve). Idealized 10⁻⁹ Torr ultimate; laminar viscous flow (no choked-flow correction at the very start). With adhesive outgassing enabled, an epoxy water reservoir at 85 °C feeds the chamber — set its exposed area A and volume V with the sliders. Flux per unit area is the diffusion-limited (Fickian) form q(t) = c₀·√(D/πt)·e^(−t/τ₁) with c₀ = 1 wt % H₂O × 1.15 g/cm³ ≈ 11.7 Torr·L per cm³ of adhesive (1 mg H₂O ≈ 1.02 Torr·L), D(85 °C) ≈ 4.5×10⁻⁸ cm²/s (epoxy-class; Arrhenius from D(295 K) = 2×10⁻⁹ cm²/s, E_a = 0.45 eV), and reservoir time constant τ₁ = 4L²/π²D at drying depth L = V/A — so the source is finite and the pump-down now shows a bake endpoint. At defaults (A = 20 cm², V = 0.5 cm³ ≈ 5.9 Torr·L of water), q(1 h) ≈ 2.4×10⁻⁵ Torr·L/s/cm² pre-depletion (the e^(−t/τ₁) factor already bites at 1 h for thin layers); the earlier hard-coded 5×10⁻⁵ was this same quantity rounded up ~×2, and its 1/t decay (a surface-desorption law) is replaced by the physically-correct 1/√t bulk-diffusion decay (Chiggiato, CERN Yellow Rep. arXiv:2006.07124; dual-stage caveats in the documentation below). What comes out of the glue is water-dominated: cured epoxies at ≤100 °C emit mostly absorbed H₂O (ASTM E595 reports Water Vapor Regained separately for exactly this reason), plus a minor organics fraction (amines/diluents — heavy, slow through the bottleneck at √(28/M), condensable: an optics-contamination concern more than a pressure one) and a little CO₂; CO on an RGA is generally the steel and the filament talking, not the glue. Two wall-water populations are also modeled: the heated chamber walls' adsorbed water (fast pool, first ~2 h at 85 °C) and the unheated manifold's 1/t tail injected on the pump side — which is why the RGA water background outlives the chamber's own dry-down. H₂O is tracked as its own species — same viscous conductance (bulk flow carries everything), molecular conductance scaled by √(29/18) ≈ 1.27 since C_mol ∝ v̄ ∝ 1/√M. Wall re-adsorption elsewhere is neglected, and the turbo is assumed to pump H₂O at full speed. With the air-leak source enabled, a constant throughput Q enters the chamber and the pump-down flattens onto the floor P = Q/S_eff — constant-forever floor vs slowly-falling floor is the classic leak-vs-outgassing discriminator (see the leak-diagnosis process doc in analysis/). Integrated with backward Euler on an adaptive log-time grid; with outgassing enabled the run extends to 10⁷ s ≈ 116 days.

Vacuum-loss documentation — who owns the gas load, and when

The sealed-dewar gas budget is brutally small: staying below the ~10⁻³ Torr soft-vacuum threshold in a 0.1 L volume is a total budget of P·V ≈ 10⁻⁴ Torr·L ≈ 0.1 µg of water, while one milligram of absorbed water is ≈ 1 Torr·L and outgassing is ~90 % of the gas load in a typical high-vacuum system (Grinham & Chew 2017, ref. 3). The caveat: water stops being the problem — CH₄ and He inherit it

With a healthy activated getter, water and hydrogen are absorbed after seal-off, so they stop being pressure problems at all — leftover water instead spends getter capacity and, during cold operation, cryopumps onto the coldest optic (water ice absorbs strongly at 3.16 / 4.6 / 6.1 / 13 µm; ~0.1 µm on a cold filter is already measurable, ref 12). The long-term pressure rise is then owned by what the getter cannot pump: methane from the steel and helium through any glass. Our representative CH₄ rate lands almost exactly on the 10-year no-getter allowance — which is the deep reason well-built gettered dewars are a "10-year-class" product — but that rate is uncertain by about a decade (hence EOL 3.5–35 yr in the bars). Helium only matters through glass windows or frits (~4×10⁻⁵ Torr per decade for a 4 cm², 1 mm borosilicate window at the atmosphere's 5.2 ppm He); metal-brazed Ge/Si/sapphire windows make it negligible (refs 9, 13). Without a getter, the story never gets that far: after any competent bake, H₂ owns the budget within days — water only re-enters the picture if the bake was cut short of the knee. Practical consequence: bake quality is measured in getter-capacity margin and ice budget, while end-of-life is measured in CH₄/He accumulation — two different currencies, verified by different tests (rate-of-rise + RGA composition vs. long-interval storage trending).

Water desorption from the metal surfaces — reviewed

Every air-exposed metal surface carries a few monolayers of water (1 monolayer = 10¹⁵ molecules/cm² ≈ 3.1×10⁻⁵ Torr·L/cm²; 3 monolayers on 300 cm² ≈ 0.027 Torr·L). Unbaked, it leaves following the famous inverse-time law q ≈ 3×10⁻⁹/t(h) mbar·L·s⁻¹·cm⁻² (refs 1, 2) — the rate depends on how long you have pumped, not on the material being "done". The physical origin is first-order desorption over a broad distribution of binding energies (~0.75–1.15 eV, attempt frequency ν ≈ 10¹³ s⁻¹): the superposition of exponentials over that distribution is the 1/t law (Redhead 1995, Li & Dylla 1993 — refs 4, 5; our implementation reproduces the empirical coefficient within ×1.5 with no fitting). Baking advances an "erosion front" E* = k_B·T·ln(ν·t): each decade of time buys only 2.3 k_B·T (~0.07 eV at 85 °C) while temperature multiplies the whole front — bake hotter beats bake longer. Worked equivalences: 85 °C × 1 day out-anneals 10 years of 22 °C storage; 60 °C needs ~13 days for the same front; covering six months of 71 °C storage soak takes ~30 days at 85 °C but only ~5 at 100 °C.

Two honest footnotes. First, measured "unbaked metal" rates scatter ~100× across sources (compare ref 3's fresh-stainless table against ref 1's 1-h value) — history and measurement method, not physics; and throughput measurements over-read water by ~3× from readsorption (ref 3). Second, our Monte Carlo says that after any competent bake the surface-water parameters barely move the answers (|rank correlation| ≤ 0.06 on required bake time) — the precision belongs on the adhesives. Where surface water does matter is pump-down behavior and RGA interpretation: this page's simulator shows the manifold-vs-chamber H₂O attenuation (~S/C) that makes the gauge read optimistic during exactly that phase.
