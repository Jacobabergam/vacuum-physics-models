Bottleneck-limited pumpdown: viscous vs. molecular flow

A roughing pump and a turbo hang off a small manifold, connected to the chamber you actually care about through a narrow tube. Every run starts at 760 Torr: the rougher takes the system to crossover, then the turbo valves in and the rougher moves to the foreline to back it. Near atmosphere the tube conducts like a fire hose and the pump is the limit; once the mean free path outgrows the tube bore, conductance collapses to a fixed geometric value and the tube — not the pump — sets how fast the chamber empties. Neither pump has a constant speed, and there is nowhere to type one in — pick the hardware and the model reads its speed off that pump's own curve at the current pressure.

Why the bottleneck stops making sense at high vacuum. Gas flow through a tube is characterized by its conductance C (L/s), and the pump speed the chamber actually feels is the series combination S_eff = (1/S + 1/C)⁻¹ — the smaller of the two always wins.
Viscous regime (λ ≪ d): molecules collide with each other constantly, so gas moves as a bulk fluid driven by pressure gradient — Poiseuille flow, C ≈ 180 · d⁴ · P̄ / L (cm, Torr → L/s). Conductance is proportional to pressure and is enormous near atmosphere, so the tube is invisible and the pump is the limit. Your fluid-flow intuition works here.
Molecular regime (λ ≳ d): a molecule crosses the whole tube without meeting another molecule. There is no "flow" to push — each molecule wanders by wall bounces and only by chance finds the exit. Conductance collapses to a pressure-independent, purely geometric value C_mol ≈ 12.1 · d³ / (L + 4d/3), which for a narrow tube falls far below the pump speed. The turbo can remove everything that arrives, but almost nothing arrives: S_eff → C_mol, no matter how big the pump is. That is why time-to-base scales so brutally with a small bore at high vacuum — and why d⁴ vs d³ matters far less than the loss of the pressure term.
Mean free path for air at 20 °C: λ = 5×10⁻³ / P cm·Torr — the classic "5 microns at 1 Torr". Regime boundaries drawn at Kn = λ/d of 0.01 (viscous) and 0.5 (molecular).
Neither pump offers a constant speed, and the reasons are opposites. Runs start at 760 Torr, so a turbo cannot be first — its blades only work in molecular flow. The rougher takes the chamber to the crossover setpoint, then the turbo valves in and the rougher is repurposed as its backing pump.
The roughing pumps are positive displacement: they sweep a fixed volume per revolution, but back-leakage across the seals grows as the inlet falls until it exactly cancels the swept volume. That cancellation is the ultimate, so the curve is S = S_peak · (1 − P_ult/P) — flat from atmosphere until roughly a decade above ultimate, then a cliff. The two options differ mostly in where that cliff sits: the nXDS10iC (3.17 L/s, ultimate 5.3×10⁻³ Torr) is still at 95 % of peak at a 100 mTorr crossover, while the larger ACP 15 (4.17 L/s, ultimate 3.8×10⁻² Torr) is already down to 62 % there. Bigger is not automatically faster at the pressure you care about.
The turbo is the mirror image. Its blades throw molecules downstream, so its 300 L/s is a capture number that stays flat wherever flow is molecular — it does not care how low the pressure gets. What bends its curve is throughput: rotor and drag stage can only shift Q_max = 115 sccm = 1.46 Torr·L/s, so above P_max = Q_max/S = 4.9×10⁻³ Torr it is throughput-limited to S = Q_max/P. At a 100 mTorr crossover that is roughly 33 L/s, about a tenth of its rating. The turbo only wakes up properly once the chamber is already in the low 10⁻³ decade.
The two stages are coupled, not independent. Everything the turbo removes has to be swallowed by the rougher, so the foreline settles where S_rough(P_fore) · P_fore = Q_turbo — solved here by bisection, because the rougher's own speed depends on that pressure. The result is checked against the nEXT300D's 7.1 Torr critical backing pressure: exceed it and the turbo stalls. At default geometry the peak foreline is about 0.47 Torr, some 7 % of budget, so either rougher backs this turbo comfortably.
Model: air at 20 °C; two lumped volumes coupled by C(P̄) = C_viscous + C_molecular (Knudsen-interpolation approximation). The rougher's ultimate lives inside its speed because back-leakage past the seals is a real loss of swept volume; the turbo's is a back-leak term instead, so its reported speed stays 300 L/s at base rather than collapsing to zero. The turbo is given the same speed for H₂O as for N₂, which is close but not exact. Crossover is latched on the chamber gauge (or when the rougher hits its own ultimate) and treated as an instantaneous valve swap; a real crossover has a finite valve transient and a turbo spin-up. The 1 − P_ult/P roll-off is the standard idealization of the published rougher curves, not a fit to digitized points. Laminar viscous flow (no choked-flow correction at the very start). With adhesive outgassing enabled, an epoxy water reservoir at 85 °C feeds the chamber — set its exposed area A and volume V with the sliders. Flux per unit area is the diffusion-limited (Fickian) form q(t) = c₀·√(D/πt)·e^(−t/τ₁) with c₀ = 1 wt % H₂O × 1.15 g/cm³ ≈ 11.7 Torr·L per cm³ of adhesive (1 mg H₂O ≈ 1.02 Torr·L), D(85 °C) ≈ 4.5×10⁻⁸ cm²/s (epoxy-class; Arrhenius from D(295 K) = 2×10⁻⁹ cm²/s, E_a = 0.45 eV), and reservoir time constant τ₁ = 4L²/π²D at drying depth L = V/A — so the source is finite and the pump-down now shows a bake endpoint. At defaults (A = 20 cm², V = 0.5 cm³ ≈ 5.9 Torr·L of water), q(1 h) ≈ 2.4×10⁻⁵ Torr·L/s/cm² pre-depletion (the e^(−t/τ₁) factor already bites at 1 h for thin layers); the earlier hard-coded 5×10⁻⁵ was this same quantity rounded up ~×2, and its 1/t decay (a surface-desorption law) is replaced by the physically-correct 1/√t bulk-diffusion decay (Chiggiato, CERN Yellow Rep. arXiv:2006.07124; dual-stage caveats in the documentation below). What comes out of the glue is water-dominated: cured epoxies at ≤100 °C emit mostly absorbed H₂O (ASTM E595 reports Water Vapor Regained separately for exactly this reason), plus a minor organics fraction (amines/diluents — heavy, slow through the bottleneck at √(28/M), condensable: an optics-contamination concern more than a pressure one) and a little CO₂; CO on an RGA is generally the steel and the filament talking, not the glue. Two wall-water populations are also modeled: the heated chamber walls' adsorbed water (fast pool, first ~2 h at 85 °C) and the unheated manifold's 1/t tail injected on the pump side — which is why the RGA water background outlives the chamber's own dry-down. H₂O is tracked as its own species — same viscous conductance (bulk flow carries everything), molecular conductance scaled by √(29/18) ≈ 1.27 since C_mol ∝ v̄ ∝ 1/√M. Wall re-adsorption elsewhere is neglected, and the turbo is assumed to pump H₂O at its N₂ speed. With the air-leak source enabled, a constant throughput Q enters the chamber and the pump-down flattens onto the floor P = Q/S_eff — constant-forever floor vs slowly-falling floor is the classic leak-vs-outgassing discriminator (see the leak-diagnosis process doc in analysis/). Integrated with backward Euler on an adaptive log-time grid; every run spans the same fixed 20-day window.

Vacuum-loss documentation — who owns the gas load, and when

The sealed-dewar gas budget is brutally small: staying below the ~10⁻³ Torr soft-vacuum threshold in a 0.1 L volume is a total budget of P·V ≈ 10⁻⁴ Torr·L ≈ 0.1 µg of water, while one milligram of absorbed water is ≈ 1 Torr·L and outgassing is ~90 % of the gas load in a typical high-vacuum system (Grinham & Chew 2017, ref. 3). Which species owns that budget changes by orders of magnitude over life — the chart and bars below are computed from the companion model in dewar_vacuum_model/ (300 cm² steel, 0.1 L, 85 °C bakes; REVIEW.md has the error budget).

The caveat: water stops being the problem — CH₄ and He inherit it

With a healthy activated getter, water and hydrogen are absorbed after seal-off, so they stop being pressure problems at all — leftover water instead spends getter capacity and, during cold operation, cryopumps onto the coldest optic (water ice absorbs strongly at 3.16 / 4.6 / 6.1 / 13 µm; ~0.1 µm on a cold filter is already measurable, ref 12). The long-term pressure rise is then owned by what the getter cannot pump: methane from the steel and helium through any glass. Our representative CH₄ rate lands almost exactly on the 10-year no-getter allowance — which is the deep reason well-built gettered dewars are a "10-year-class" product — but that rate is uncertain by about a decade (hence EOL 3.5–35 yr in the bars). Helium only matters through glass windows or frits (~4×10⁻⁵ Torr per decade for a 4 cm², 1 mm borosilicate window at the atmosphere's 5.2 ppm He); metal-brazed Ge/Si/sapphire windows make it negligible (refs 9, 13). Without a getter, the story never gets that far: after any competent bake, H₂ owns the budget within days — water only re-enters the picture if the bake was cut short of the knee. Practical consequence: bake quality is measured in getter-capacity margin and ice budget, while end-of-life is measured in CH₄/He accumulation — two different currencies, verified by different tests (rate-of-rise + RGA composition vs. long-interval storage trending).

Water desorption from the metal surfaces — reviewed

Every air-exposed metal surface carries a few monolayers of water (1 monolayer = 10¹⁵ molecules/cm² ≈ 3.1×10⁻⁵ Torr·L/cm²; 3 monolayers on 300 cm² ≈ 0.027 Torr·L). Unbaked, it leaves following the famous inverse-time law q ≈ 3×10⁻⁹/t(h) mbar·L·s⁻¹·cm⁻² (refs 1, 2) — the rate depends on how long you have pumped, not on the material being "done". The physical origin is first-order desorption over a broad distribution of binding energies (~0.75–1.15 eV, attempt frequency ν ≈ 10¹³ s⁻¹): the superposition of exponentials over that distribution is the 1/t law (Redhead 1995, Li & Dylla 1993 — refs 4, 5; our implementation reproduces the empirical coefficient within ×1.5 with no fitting). Baking advances an "erosion front" E* = k_B·T·ln(ν·t): each decade of time buys only 2.3 k_B·T (~0.07 eV at 85 °C) while temperature multiplies the whole front — bake hotter beats bake longer. Worked equivalences: 85 °C × 1 day out-anneals 10 years of 22 °C storage; 60 °C needs ~13 days for the same front; covering six months of 71 °C storage soak takes ~30 days at 85 °C but only ~5 at 100 °C.

Two honest footnotes. First, measured "unbaked metal" rates scatter ~100× across sources (compare ref 3's fresh-stainless table against ref 1's 1-h value) — history and measurement method, not physics; and throughput measurements over-read water by ~3× from readsorption (ref 3). Second, our Monte Carlo says that after any competent bake the surface-water parameters barely move the answers (|rank correlation| ≤ 0.06 on required bake time) — the precision belongs on the adhesives. Where surface water does matter is pump-down behavior and RGA interpretation: this page's simulator shows the manifold-vs-chamber H₂O attenuation (~S/C) that makes the gauge read optimistic during exactly that phase.
